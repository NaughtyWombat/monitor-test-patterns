Monitor Test Patterns

Thirteen test patterns that fill the whole display, for checking a display for dead or stuck pixels, gradient banding, blurry text, uneven backlight and crushed blacks. Everything runs in your browser. There is nothing to install and nothing is uploaded.

Choose a test pattern

Click a pattern to open it fullscreen. Use the arrow keys, or tap the right side of the display, to move to the next one.

What each pattern checks

PatternWhat to look for
WhiteDark dots are dead pixels. Also look for dim corners, yellow tint or a dirty, blotchy look.
BlackBright dots are stuck pixels. In a dark room, light leaking from the edges is backlight bleed; a silvery haze that changes with viewing angle is IPS glow.
RedAny pixel that is not pure red. A black dot means the red subpixel is dead.
GreenAny pixel that is not pure green. Green is the brightest channel, so defects stand out here.
BlueAny pixel that is not pure blue.
50% grayThe whole panel should be one even gray. Patches, vertical bands or a color tint in one area mean poor uniformity.
Gray rampA smooth fade from black to white. Visible steps or bands suggest 6-bit color, heavy processing or a calibration issue.
Color rampsRed, green, blue and gray ramps should all be smooth. Banding in only one channel points to a color-processing problem.
Black levelTwenty blocks from RGB 1 to 20 on pure black. On a well-set display you can tell most of them apart; if the first several look identical, blacks are being crushed.
White levelTwenty blocks from RGB 235 to 254 on pure white. If the brightest blocks blend into the background, highlights are clipped; lower the contrast setting.
1px checkerboardShould look like an even, flat gray from a distance. Moiré, blur or shimmering means the image is being scaled or the panel has pixel inversion artifacts.
GridLines should be straight and evenly spaced, and the outer border should be fully visible. A missing edge means overscan is cropping the picture.
Color barsSeven bars: white, yellow, cyan, green, magenta, red, blue. Each should look clean and saturated without bleeding into its neighbors.

How to check a monitor with these patterns

  1. Prepare the panel. Let it warm up for about 15 minutes. Wipe off dust so you don't mistake a speck for a dead pixel, and set the display to its native resolution with scaling at 100%.
  2. Darken the room for the black, near-black and uniformity patterns. Backlight bleed and IPS glow are easy to miss in daylight.
  3. Go through the solid colors one at a time and scan the whole panel in sections. A pixel that stays black on white is dead, and one that stays red, green or blue on black is stuck.
  4. Check the gradients and steps. Smooth ramps that show bands, or near-black steps that merge together, usually point to a low bit depth, aggressive dynamic contrast or a wrong gamma setting.
  5. Finish with the fine patterns. The 1-pixel checkerboard and grid only look crisp when the image maps exactly to physical pixels, so blur or moiré here means scaling is involved.

Patterns are drawn at your display's device pixel ratio, so 1-pixel lines really are one physical pixel wide on high-DPI laptops and phones.

Keyboard shortcuts

  • → Space or tap the right half: next pattern
  • ← or tap the left half: previous pattern
  • F: toggle browser fullscreen
  • H: show or hide the pattern name
  • Esc: close the pattern

Each pattern also has its own address, such as #black or #gray-ramp, so you can bookmark one or send it to someone.

More display tests

These patterns are for quick visual checks. If you need guided tests with explanations for each step, try the free online monitor test at monitortest.cc. It also has a refresh rate test that measures the Hz your browser is really getting, and a burn-in test for OLED and plasma panels.

About this project

Monitor Test Patterns is a single, dependency-free HTML file released under the MIT license. You can save it and use it offline, or put it on a USB stick for checking displays in a shop. The source code, issue tracker and pattern suggestions live on GitHub.
